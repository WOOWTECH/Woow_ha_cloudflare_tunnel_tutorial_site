Chapter 10

Logs and routine maintenance

You do not have to understand every line of the log to judge whether the tunnel is healthy: the time, the severity and any repeating pattern are enough. You will learn to watch the log live, filter it, download it and tidy it up safely, and to settle into a maintenance rhythm that does not disturb the services at home.

Why this matters

When remote access breaks, the browser only tells you it "cannot open the page", while the real cause may sit in DNS, the Cloudflare edge, cloudflared or Home Assistant. A log is like the visitor log by your front door: you do not have to memorize every entry. Look at the time the failure happened, then find the warnings or errors that repeat around that time, and you narrow the search.

The Logs page in the Cloudflared Web GUI shows a live stream of the add-on log. It comes from the same source as the Log tab of the Home Assistant add-on, and it adds text filtering and download. This page is for watching and collecting evidence, not a permanent audit system: after you reopen it, clear the screen, or the buffer rotates, older lines may no longer be there.

Note the time first: write down which URL, which device, and roughly what time it failed, then read the log around that time. That is far faster than reading from the first line to the last.

Five ideas to understand about logs

Two kinds of verbosity: log_level controls the add-on's own messages; the verbosity of the cloudflared tunnel itself comes from the allowed run_parameters (for example --loglevel=debug). They are not the same switch.
Color is only a hint: the Web GUI colors a line by the words error, fatal, warn and debug it contains, so you still have to read the whole line and what surrounds it. A single warning is not necessarily a failure; the same error appearing again and again is what deserves a look.

Run one safe log review

  1. Confirm local Home Assistant is healthy first

    On your home network, open the local Home Assistant URL and check that you can sign in and use it. Note the current time, and keep a second device ready with Wi-Fi off and mobile data on. Do not change any settings yet.

  2. Open the Cloudflared Logs page

    Open Cloudflared from the Home Assistant sidebar, then select Logs. It normally connects on its own; if the status is Disconnected, press Connect. New lines arriving only means the log stream works — tunnel health still needs its own acceptance check.

  3. Turn off auto-scroll and find the time

    Turn Auto-scroll off and go back to around the time you noted. Read the few lines before and after the error line, and work out which stage it came from: preparing the configuration, opening edge connections, setting up DNS, or reaching the Home Assistant origin.

  4. Filter with one short word at a time

    In Filter, enter error first, then try fatal and warn separately. Do not paste the three words in together: this is a single substring match, not an advanced query language. Clear the Filter when you need the full context.

  5. Reproduce it once from outside

    Open your external hostname over mobile data, and note the result and the time. Go back to Logs and check whether new errors appear in the same window, then compare the add-on and edge connection status on the Dashboard. One test is enough; refreshing rapidly just creates noise.

  6. Decide on full or trimmed before you download

    When you are handing it to a maintainer to analyze, clear the Filter and then press Download, so the context is kept; keep the Filter on only when you want one kind of message. After downloading, check the file in a text editor and redact the hostname, account, IP, tunnel ID, authorization URL, token and any other detail about your local network before you share it.

Do not mistake a walkthrough for something already done: while the tutorial environment for this site was being made we only looked, read-only: we pressed no Save, Save & Restart, Restart or Authorize, and changed no tunnel. When you press Save & Restart in your own Config, it really does write to Supervisor options and restart the add-on, interrupting the tunnel briefly. Do not press it without a maintenance window and a backup of the original settings.

How to plan routine maintenance

ScenarioRecommended practiceWhat not to do
Everything works normallyStay on the default info, and run one quick local, external and Dashboard acceptance check every month, or after your network equipment changes.Leave debug or trace on permanently, piling up information you do not need and sensitive context.
Occasional disconnectionsNote the time, download the unfiltered log, then compare it against your network or the Cloudflare status. Evidence comes before a restart.Restart again and again the moment you see an error, washing away the order things happened in.
You need more add-on detailRaise log_level temporarily during a maintenance window, reproduce the problem once, then put it back immediately.Assume it must also add messages from cloudflared itself.
You need more tunnel detailUse the allowed --loglevel=debug only as the add-on documentation describes; record the original value first, then remove or restore it when you are done.Paste in parameters from an unknown source, or hand anyone the full command line and the token.
You are about to update the add-onRead the add-on changelog, create a Home Assistant backup, and keep a redacted summary of the settings together with a passing acceptance result.Update while this is your only remaining remote connection and you have no local way to recover.
The minimum maintenance record: keep the date, the Home Assistant version, the add-on version, whether the mode is locally managed or remotely managed, the result, and a redacted summary of the errors. That is enough. A token and a Cloudflare authorization URL never belong in a maintenance record.

Security checks before you download or share

Check itemPassing standardWhy
Authorization dataNo Cloudflare authorization URL, tunnel_token, TunnelSecret, certificate content or full command line.Whoever obtains them may gain authorization, or attach a connector to your tunnel.

The Web GUI redacts some secrets, but that does not replace checking by hand: the project redacts known tunnel tokens and some secret formats, but unknown formats, hostnames, IPs and the content of your own services can still appear. Treat every downloaded file as sensitive data.

Acceptance tests after maintenance

  1. The local entrance passes

    On the same home network you can open Home Assistant, sign in and load the dashboard, which confirms Home Assistant itself is not the source of the failure.

  2. The external entrance passes

    With Wi-Fi off on your phone, open https://your-hostname; you reach the Cloudflare or Home Assistant sign-in flow you expect, and nothing is routed to another internal service.

  3. The Dashboard passes

    The Cloudflared Dashboard shows the add-on running and the tunnel holding an edge connection. The connection count moves around, so do not treat one fixed number as the only standard.

  4. The Logs page passes

    Logs shows Connected, new lines arrive during the test, and no single fatal/error keeps cycling. Judge a brief warning together with whether the feature actually works.

  5. The security state passes

    Your router still has no inbound port open for Home Assistant as a result of this guide; the temporary debug / trace setting is back to normal, and the downloaded file is redacted and moved somewhere controlled.

  6. The record passes

    Write down the test date, the versions, the mode and the result. Do not record tokens, authorization URLs or credentials anyone could use directly. If you changed something, write down how to undo it as well.

How to judge the acceptance check: confirm all of these at once — it really works, nothing goes to the wrong service, no error repeats, and nothing extra is exposed. A green status on its own is not a complete acceptance check.

Common Logs page pitfalls

  1. It stays on Disconnected

    Press Connect first, then reload the Cloudflared Ingress page. Check that your Home Assistant sign-in is still valid; if the Dashboard will not open either, look at the add-on's Home Assistant Log tab. Disconnected describes the log WebSocket, and on its own it is not enough to conclude that the tunnel is down.

  2. Filtering leaves the view completely empty

    Clear the Filter, confirm the original lines are still there, then switch to one shorter keyword. Filter is a case-insensitive "contains" match; it does not support AND, OR or regular expressions, and a single typo can leave you with no results.

  3. The downloaded file is missing the context you wanted

    The current interface downloads what the Log Viewer is showing. If a Filter was on, you may get only the matching lines: clear the Filter, reproduce the problem again, then download. Lines old enough may already have left the memory buffer, so cross-check the HA add-on Log when you need to.

  4. Thinking Clear deleted the system record

    Clear only removes the messages this browser has accumulated, and new lines keep arriving; it does not clear Supervisor's log storage. When you need to keep evidence, download it before you press Clear or restart.

  5. You raised the log level and see no difference

    Tell the add-on's log_level and cloudflared's --loglevel apart first. A settings change usually takes effect only after the add-on restarts: Save writes the value, and only Save & Restart writes it and restarts. That is a real change, so back up first, schedule a maintenance window and record the original value.

Collect evidence before you restart: Restart interrupts the current tunnel, and it can push the most important pre-startup context off the screen. When you are managing remotely with no local fallback, do not make a restart your first step.

FAQ

Are Logs and the Home Assistant add-on Log tab two separate records?
No. The project documentation explains that the Web GUI streams the add-on's own log, the same content as the HA Log tab; the Web GUI adds live streaming, filtering and download on top. The point in time each side shows, and how many lines it keeps, can differ depending on when the page was open.
Does a red line mean the tunnel is broken?
Not necessarily. The color is a hint taken from the words in the line. Look at that line's time, its context and whether it repeats, then verify through the external entrance and the Dashboard. A single old error does not mean it is still failing now.
Can I leave debug on permanently to make later troubleshooting easier?
We do not recommend it. Verbose logs add noise and widen the exposure of sensitive information. Day to day, use the info level the documentation recommends; when you need more, raise it briefly, reproduce the problem once, download, redact, then put it back.
Can I paste a whole log into a GitHub issue?
Not as it is. Make a copy first and remove the authorization URL, tokens, secrets, accounts, hostnames, tunnel IDs, IPs and internal service data, keeping only the time window that reproduces the problem. If you suspect a token has leaked, rotate or revoke it on the Cloudflare side before you deal with what is public.
Do Clear and Disconnect stop the add-on?
No. Clear removes the lines the browser has accumulated; Disconnect stops the live stream on this page. Neither one is a Restart. Confirm the tunnel status on the Dashboard and through the external entrance as usual.
