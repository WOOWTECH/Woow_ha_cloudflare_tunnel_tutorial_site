Chapter 12

Troubleshooting, updates and handover

Work inward from what your phone shows you: DNS, the Cloudflare edge, the tunnel connector, cloudflared and the Home Assistant origin. You will also learn to leave yourself a way back before an update, to roll back safely when one fails, and to hand over a record that carries no secrets but is still enough for the next person to take over.

Why this matters

When the remote URL will not open, the most common failure is not that "the whole thing is broken" — it is that one layer failed to connect. Reinstalling the add-on, changing DNS or resetting the token as your first move is like pulling the breaker box apart the moment the power trips at home: the evidence disappears, and you may add a second problem on top of the first.

This chapter works outside in: first save the error the user actually saw, then check the hostname and the Cloudflare edge, then the tunnel connector and the add-on log, and finally verify the local Home Assistant origin. Each step makes one decision. When you find the first layer that fails, stop there and deal with it.

The troubleshooting trio: note the time it happened, the full error code or the text on the screen, and whether you were on Wi-Fi or mobile data at the time. Do not just write "it does not work", and do not attach the authorization URL, a token, or a whole log you have not redacted.

A status is not a conclusion: an edge connection on the Dashboard only means the connector has a link to the Cloudflare edge. It does not prove that DNS points to the right place, that the Access policy is correct, or that the origin Home Assistant is usable.

Work through it once, outside in

  1. Fix the test conditions and save the symptom

    Turn Wi-Fi off on your phone and open the full https://hostname over mobile data. Test once only, and note the time, the error code, and whether you reached Cloudflare Access or the HA sign-in. If the page shows identifying information, write it down as text; you do not need a screenshot.

  2. Check the hostname and DNS

    Confirm the URL has no typo, no extra space and no wrong subdomain. In your own Cloudflare DNS, do a read-only check that the record exists and points at the tunnel you expect. A locally managed add-on manages the matching DNS; for remotely managed routes, the Cloudflare dashboard is what counts.

  3. Work out whether the Cloudflare edge has taken over

    If you get a Cloudflare-branded error page, save the full error code. For 1033, check the connector first; for 502, check whether the origin is reachable. If Access denied you, look at the identity policy first, and do not change the tunnel token.

  4. Check the tunnel and add-on status

    In the Cloudflare dashboard, look at the matching tunnel connector, then open the Web GUI Dashboard to see the add-on and its edge connections. Locally managed and remotely managed tunnels are administered in different places, but both need at least one healthy connector before they can carry traffic.

  5. Compare the Logs from the same window

    Open Logs, clear the Filter first, and find the time from step one. Then filter separately on error, warn, or a non-sensitive word from the error. Download and redact before you consider a restart. Disconnected only means the log stream dropped; it is not a conclusion about the tunnel.

  6. Check the Home Assistant origin last

    Go back to your home network and open the local HA URL. If it fails locally too, fix HA first. If it works locally but you get a 502 from outside, check the HA port and SSL the add-on detected, the http: settings and the trusted proxy. Change one thing at a time, and retest from the outermost layer after each change.

We fixed nothing in the tutorial environment: while making this guide we only looked, read-only. We did not press Save, Save & Restart, Restart, Authorize or Delete, and we changed no DNS, tunnel or HA setting. When you press Save & Restart, it really does write to Supervisor options and restart the add-on, and existing remote connections drop briefly. Do not run it without a local entrance, a backup and a maintenance window.

Choosing between updating and rolling back

ScenarioBefore the updateConservative rollback if it fails
Changing add-on options onlySave the original values, redacted, together with a passing acceptance result, and confirm you can sign in to HA locally.Put the single change back to the value you know was good. Save only stores it; only Save & Restart applies it and restarts.
Updating the Cloudflared Web GUI add-onRead the add-on changelog, create a Home Assistant backup that includes the add-on settings, and record the current version.Prefer restoring from the HA backup you made before the update and verified as working. Do not assume the add-on store always offers a one-click downgrade.
Updating Home AssistantRead the official release notes and breaking changes, create a backup, and check the compatibility information for the Cloudflared add-on.Follow the official Home Assistant backup and restore procedure, and set aside time for local work and downtime.
Changing DNS or the public hostnameRecord the old record, the route and the origin, but leave the token out. Work out first who manages the tunnel — locally managed or remotely managed.Restore the last record you are sure of, wait for the DNS cache to refresh, and only then test. Do not rebuild the tunnel at the same time.
Changing a Cloudflare Access policyKeep a working administrator and a local HA entrance, and record the old behavior with an allow and a deny test case.Go back to the last minimal working policy rather than opening it to everyone, then run the acceptance check from both identity directions.
A token you think may have leakedThis is not an ordinary update.Rotate or revoke it on the Cloudflare side, review the connectors, DNS and account activity, then update the protected field safely.
cloudflared does not update itself inside the container: the add-on documentation lists no-autoupdate among the default startup parameters. Version updates should follow the add-on releases and images. Do not go into the container and swap the binary by hand, or the next rebuild will not be reliable either.
Change one layer at a time: roll the settings back first and run the acceptance check. If it still fails, then consider rolling back the version. Change DNS, Access, the token and the add-on all at once and you will never know which action did anything.

How to write a safe handover pack

The point of a handover is to let the next maintainer know "how the system connects, where it is administered, and how to run the acceptance check and roll back". It is not to pack every secret into one document. The material below is enough to work through most problems.

Handover itemSafe to recordNever include
Scope and ownersWhich role maintains Home Assistant, the Cloudflare account and DNS, and how to reach someone in an emergency.Shared account passwords, MFA seeds, recovery codes.
VersionsThe HA Core, OS and Supervisor versions, the Cloudflared Web GUI add-on version, and the date of the last acceptance check.A full system diagnostics bundle you have not redacted.
Tunnel modeLocally managed or remotely managed, the tunnel display name, and what each hostname is for.tunnel_token, TunnelSecret, the contents of the credentials file, the Cloudflare authorization URL.
Route summaryWhat the external hostname and each necessary additional host are used for, the type of origin and who owns it.Private IPs nobody needs, admin ports, the local network topology, device passwords.
What normal looks likeThe local and external test steps, the sign-in flow you expect, and roughly what the Dashboard and Logs should show.Replayable sessions, cookies, or unredacted screenshots of a sign-in.
Rollback planThe backup date and its controlled storage location, a summary of the last good version and settings, and who can approve a restore.The backup decryption key and the backup file in the same public document.
Known issuesThe time it happened, the error code, the steps already tried, and redacted log excerpts.A whole raw debug or trace log.
Hand over secrets on a separate channel: if the person taking over genuinely needs a token or recovery codes, use the password manager your organization approves, with the least access that works. Do not tuck them into the handover document, an email, a chat log or an issue. When the handover is done, remove the access the previous owner no longer needs.

Full acceptance check after an update or rollback

  1. Versions and settings match

    Confirm the add-on version you actually have matches the plan, and that Config contains only approved changes. The locally managed or remotely managed mode has not switched by accident, and the remote token field appears in none of your records.

  2. Local HA is healthy

    On your home network you can sign in, load the dashboard and perform one risk-free read-only action. The HA log is not rejecting requests over and over because of the proxy settings.

  3. The connector and Logs are healthy

    The Dashboard shows the add-on running and the tunnel with an edge connection. Logs connects, and after startup there is no fatal or error loop repeating. The connection count can vary, so do not expect a fixed number.

  4. The external entrance works

    Over mobile data your phone reaches the Access or HA sign-in you expect, and the basic pages work once you are signed in. Nothing routes to the wrong additional host.

  5. The deny path works

    A hostname that does not exist, or that you removed, does not surface an internal service through wildcard or catch-all. If you use Access, a test account that is not allowed really is denied.

  6. The maintenance wrap-up is clean

    Temporary debug settings are back to normal, downloaded logs are redacted, no inbound port was added to the router, the pre-update backup is stored under your retention policy, and the handover record has the date and the result written into it.

Accept or roll back: close it out only when all six pass. If any one fails, save the evidence, roll back the last action according to your change plan, then run the whole check again. Do not treat "mostly working" as done.

The order to work through common failures

  1. Error 1033: no healthy tunnel connector

    Look at the Cloudflare Tunnel connectors first, then the Web GUI Dashboard and the add-on Logs. Check whether the add-on started, and whether your home network can reach out at all. Save the log first; restart once only when you have a local entrance and approval. Do not rebuild the DNS or create a second tunnel with the same name.

  2. Cloudflare 502: the connector cannot reach the origin

    Test the local HA URL from your home network first. If it fails locally, fix HA. If it works locally, check the origin protocol and port, the HA SSL setting and the http: settings. Look through the cloudflared and HA logs from the same window for connection refused, TLS or proxy clues.

  3. It works from outside, but the Web GUI Logs shows Disconnected

    This is usually the log WebSocket on the Ingress page, not a dropped tunnel. Press Connect or reload the page, and confirm your HA session is still valid. If it still fails, switch to the Home Assistant add-on Log, and keep the time the browser showed.

  4. After an update the add-on will not start, or behaves differently

    Record the old and new versions and the first error, read the add-on changelog, and check that the options schema is not incompatible. Do not clear data or reinstall straight away. If the rollback conditions are met, restore from the HA backup you made before the update, then run the acceptance check.

  5. You still see the old DNS result after rolling back

    DNS, and the cache on your device or at your ISP, does not always update immediately. Confirm the authoritative record in the Cloudflare dashboard first, then wait a reasonable cache interval and test from a different network. Do not keep editing the record because one device is holding an old cache.

  6. The person taking over has only remote access, makes a wrong change and loses contact

    Stop trying. Ask a contact who is on the local network and authorized to sign in to HA with the handover pack, confirm the local state and the last change first, then work through the approved rollback plan. This is exactly why you verify the local entrance and the owner before an update.

Never post secrets when you ask for help in public: the version, the time, the error code, the mode, the steps you tried and a redacted excerpt are enough. The authorization URL, tokens, the tunnel ID, accounts, hostnames, IPs and full logs must all be removed. If you have already posted them, revoke or rotate immediately, depending on what was in them.

FAQ

Can I press Restart as my first step when something breaks?
We do not recommend it. Note the time and the error code first, download and redact the log, then work out which layer is at fault. Restart interrupts the tunnel, and it can push the context that matters off the screen. Do it once only when the connector problem is known to be temporary, you have a local recovery path, and it has been approved.
What is the difference between Save and Save & Restart?
According to the Web GUI interface text, Save writes the options through Supervisor, but a setting usually takes effect only after a restart. Save & Restart saves and then restarts the add-on, and the GUI drops briefly. Neither is a read-only action, so be sure you know the difference, and have a backup, before you press either.
Does updating the add-on update cloudflared as well?
The cloudflared version comes from the add-on image. The default run parameters the documentation lists include no-autoupdate, so do not expect it to update itself inside the container. Follow the add-on releases, the changelog and the Home Assistant add-on update procedure.
Is backing up the YAML text enough for a full rollback?
No. A settings summary helps when you restore by hand, but the add-on version, the protected data and the Home Assistant state are not all inside a block of YAML. Before a major update, use the official HA backup feature and confirm the backup includes the add-on data you need. Changes to Cloudflare DNS or Access also need a redacted change record of their own.
What is the minimum I should provide when I ask for help?
Give the HA and add-on versions, whether you are locally managed or remotely managed, the time of the failure, the full external error code, whether the local side is healthy, roughly what the Dashboard shows, the steps you tried, and a small redacted log excerpt. Do not give out tokens, the authorization URL, secrets, the full hostname, the tunnel ID, IPs or unredacted attachments.
