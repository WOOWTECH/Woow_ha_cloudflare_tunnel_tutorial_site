Chapter 11

Security checks and minimal exposure

A tunnel saves you from opening a router port, but that does not mean every hostname should be public. You will work through the layers one at a time — the Cloudflare account, DNS, tunnel routes, Zero Trust Access, Home Assistant sign-in and the trusted proxy — so that each layer holds only the permissions it needs to do its job.

Why this matters

With Cloudflare Tunnel the cloudflared at home dials out to Cloudflare, so you never have to open an inbound port on the router for Home Assistant. But "no open port" only solves part of the network-entrance problem: DNS still decides which name the outside world can find, the tunnel route decides which local service that name is sent to, and sign-in and Access policy decide who gets in.

Putting the NAS, the router and the camera admin page all into additional_hosts is like fitting an external door to every room. Even if every one of them asks for a sign-in, you have added entrances to update, watch and protect. The safest starting point is to expose only the Home Assistant hostname you genuinely need remotely, and to keep every other service on the local network.

Minimal exposure: before you add a hostname, answer this first: "who needs it, when they need it, whether a Home Assistant feature could do the same job, and who deletes the DNS record and the route once it is no longer used." If you cannot answer, do not add it yet.

Six boundaries, each doing one job

BoundaryWhat it coversLeast-privilege practice
Domain registrar and Cloudflare accountControl over the domain and the zone.Use an individual account that is not shared, a unique password and multi-factor authentication (MFA); keep the number of administrators as small as possible.
Cloudflare DNSPoints a hostname at the tunnel.Keep only the records still in use; after you remove a route, check for a leftover DNS record too.
Cloudflare TunnelSends the requests the edge receives to the origin you named.Create only the public hostnames you need; avoid a catch-all or a wildcard you have no use for.
Cloudflare Zero Trust AccessApplies an identity policy before the request reaches the origin.Start with an explicit allow list and a test account; do not use an over-wide domain rule or an everyone rule.
Home AssistantUser sign-in, MFA, permissions and what people can do in the app.One account per person, no shared administrator; enable MFA on accounts in use.
Ingress / Web GUILets a user already signed in to Home Assistant open the add-on GUI.The Web GUI opens no extra port; limit who holds a Home Assistant administrator account, and sign out when you leave a shared device.
The layers add up; they do not replace each other: Cloudflare Access will not manage your Home Assistant users for you, and Home Assistant MFA will not clean up a spare DNS record or tunnel route. Check every layer on its own.
Three things you never share: the Cloudflare authorization URL, the tunnel token and the secrets inside a log. The authorization URL can carry someone into a one-time authorization flow; the tunnel_token of a remotely managed setup lets whoever holds it run that tunnel; a debug log can contain environment secrets too. Do not paste any of them into an issue, a chat room or a screenshot.

Run one read-only security inventory

  1. List the external entrances you actually need

    Read external_hostname and the purpose of every additional host out of Config in the Cloudflared Web GUI without changing anything, and do not copy the token. Against each name note the user, the service and the reason for keeping it; anything with no owner or no purpose goes on the list to remove. Do not press Save yet.

  2. Work out whether you are locally managed or remotely managed

    Check whether tunnel_token is set. With a token you are remotely managed: the add-on documentation states that the other local options are ignored, and routes belong in the Cloudflare dashboard. Only a locally managed setup with no token has the add-on build the configuration and the DNS from its options. Do not guess your way through changes in both places at once.

  3. Cross-check Cloudflare DNS against the tunnel routes

    Sign in to your own Cloudflare dashboard and compare the DNS records, the tunnel public hostnames and your list, read-only. A healthy result is one hostname you need to one origin you expect, with no old names, no near-miss spellings, no unknown wildcard and no catch-all you cannot account for.

  4. Check the accounts and MFA

    In the Cloudflare account and in Home Assistant user settings, confirm who the administrators are, whether they still need the permission, and whether MFA is on. Menu names change between versions, so go in through the account's Security / Authentication pages, and never type a password into a shortcut offered by a page you do not recognize.

  5. Check the Zero Trust Access boundary

    If you use Access, find the matching self-hosted application in the Zero Trust dashboard and read every Allow, Block or other policy in turn. Confirm that what is allowed is a named person or group, and that a working recovery administrator exists. If you have not deployed Access, do not let "we have a tunnel" turn into "we are protected by Access" in your notes.

  6. Confirm the Home Assistant proxy settings are not too wide

    Check use_x_forwarded_for and trusted_proxies against the add-on documentation and the official Home Assistant HTTP documentation. Trust only the addresses your reverse proxy actually comes from; do not trust every address because it is convenient. A non-standard installation may have a different source range, so confirm it from the Home Assistant log and your own network design.

This section is an inventory, not a change: while this tutorial environment was being built we only looked, read-only: no Save, no Save & Restart, no Authorize, no Delete, and no DNS or tunnel change. When you press Save & Restart in your own Web GUI it writes to Supervisor options and then restarts the add-on, which interrupts the service briefly; deleting a DNS record, an Access policy or a token can cut the remote entrance off immediately. Back up first, confirm you can still sign in locally, pick a maintenance window, and only then carry it out separately.

How to judge high-risk options

Option or practiceExposureConservative choice
external_hostnamePuts Home Assistant on the external name you choose.Use one clear, dedicated hostname and keep the Home Assistant sign-in and MFA; confirm the router has not opened a separate Home Assistant port.
additional_hostsEvery entry can push another local service into the tunnel.Add none by default. Do not expose a service that has no sound sign-in, no update path and no audit trail of its own.
catch_all_serviceRequests that match none of the earlier rules go to the service you name.Leave it blank if you are an ordinary home user; plan for it only once you understand reverse proxy routing and default-deny behavior.
nginx_proxy_managerHands unmatched traffic to the Nginx Proxy Manager add-on.It is not an "automatically safer" switch; do not turn it on without an existing reverse proxy design.
Wildcard DNSA large number of subdomains you never listed can resolve to the tunnel as well.Avoid it; create the names you need one at a time instead, which keeps both the inventory and the removal clear.
tunnel_tokenSwitches you to remotely managed, and the other local tunnel options are ignored.Store it as you would a password and paste it only into the protected token field; rotate it on the Cloudflare side if it leaks.
post_quantumRestricts the tunnel to QUIC with post-quantum hybrid key exchange, which some networks cannot pass.Do not treat it as your one security switch; if you want it, arrange a test you can roll back from first.
Removing a route does not clear the DNS: the add-on documentation says this plainly — once you delete a hostname from additional_hosts that route stops serving, but the Cloudflare DNS record still has to be checked and deleted by hand. It is finished only when both sides are done.

Repeat every quarter: the people you live with, the devices and the temporary services all change. Running the list again every three months, and after every hostname you add, is more reliable than trying to remember the settings once something has gone wrong.

Verify from both the allow and the deny side

  1. Confirm the local recovery path

    On your home network, sign in to Home Assistant using the local URL and confirm that at least one authorized administrator can work in it. This step leaves you a way back if an external policy is set wrong.

  2. Confirm the hostnames you expect

    Turn Wi-Fi off on your phone and test each hostname on the list you decided to keep, in turn. The Home Assistant name must land on Home Assistant, or on the Access flow you designed, and never on another local service by mistake.

  3. Confirm the names that should not exist

    Pick one test subdomain you have removed, or never set up, and confirm that no wildcard or catch-all makes it show a local service by accident. Do not sweep for them with a scanning tool.

  4. Verify the identity policy

    If you use Cloudflare Access, test with one allowed account and one account outside the allow list. The first should pass the authentication you expect and the second should be blocked by Access; then confirm that Home Assistant still asks for a sign-in of its own.

  5. Verify the source addresses and the log

    Once you are signed in, look at the relevant security events or the log in Home Assistant and confirm there is no reverse proxy error and no repeated sign-in anomaly. If every connection is treated as coming from an implausible source, go back to the proxy settings; do not widen them into trusting every address.

  6. Verify that no new exposure was added

    Confirm that this guide changed no router port forwarding, that Config holds nothing you did not approve, and that debug is off. Then write the date and what passed into your maintenance record, and keep every secret out of it.

What counts as passing: the people who should get in reach the right service, the people who should not — and the names that do not exist — are blocked, and local management still works. Testing only that you yourself can sign in misses everything you have allowed too widely.

Common security-setting pitfalls

  1. You turned Access on and now you cannot get in either

    Use the local Home Assistant entrance first to confirm the service itself is fine, then have a Cloudflare administrator check the application hostname, the order of the policies, the allowed identities and the sign-in method. Do not create a permanent allow-everyone rule just to get back in; make the smallest fix you can, using a named administrator.

  2. The DNS record is still there after you deleted an additional host

    This matches the reminder in the add-on documentation: dropping a route does not delete an existing DNS record for you. Look the name up in Cloudflare DNS, confirm nothing else uses it, remove it by hand, then verify from outside that it no longer reaches the service.

  3. You set tunnel_token and the other Config items do nothing

    In remotely managed mode the other locally managed options are ignored. Manage the public hostname and the origin in the Cloudflare dashboard, and do not keep pressing Save on both sides. The token never goes into a log, a support conversation or a screenshot.

  4. Home Assistant says a request from the reverse proxy was rejected

    Check use_x_forwarded_for, trusted_proxies and where they sit in the YAML against the Home Assistant HTTP documentation and the add-on documentation. For a standard HAOS install the add-on documentation gives an example for its Docker network; a non-standard install has to be confirmed from the real source, and you must not copy in a wider range.

  5. Some services stopped working after you removed the wildcard

    Work out which hostnames were quietly relying on the wildcard, decide one by one whether to keep or retire each of them, and give the ones you need an explicit DNS record and an explicit tunnel route. Putting the wildcard back is quick, but it widens the exposure again; use it only inside an approved, short-lived rollback plan.

If a token or authorization data may have leaked: do not simply delete the post. Revoke or rotate the matching certificate or token on the Cloudflare side right away, check the tunnel connectors, the account activity and DNS for changes you do not recognize, then update the add-on's protected field and run the acceptance check.

FAQ

I use Cloudflare Tunnel — do I still need Home Assistant MFA?
Yes. The tunnel handles the network path; it is not a second factor for a Home Assistant account. Keep Home Assistant MFA, one account per person and the smallest set of administrator rights. Adding Cloudflare Access on top gives you one more layer, not a replacement.
Does the Web GUI's internal port 8099 need to be opened on the router?
No. The WoowTech documentation explains that the Web GUI is served through Home Assistant Ingress, exposes no extra port, and is protected by the Home Assistant sign-in. Do not create router port forwarding of your own.
Is the hostname itself a secret?
A public DNS name can usually be discovered from outside, so it cannot serve as a password. You should still avoid spreading a name that identifies your home through screenshots and logs that do not need it; what genuinely has to stay secret is the token, the authorization URL, the certificate and any other secret.
Do I have to turn Cloudflare Access on?
It is an extra identity boundary you can add, and whether it fits depends on how you sign in and who your users are. Until the policy and both the allow and the deny test are done, do not claim to be protected by Access; and even with Access, the Home Assistant sign-in and MFA still cannot be skipped.
Why do you advise against exposing a router or NAS admin page?
A tunnel opens no inbound port, but it still brings the application entrance out to the edge of the internet. An older admin page may have no MFA, may be updated slowly, or may hand out too much privilege. Meet a remote-access need with a purpose-built secure method first, rather than listing every local admin surface in additional_hosts.
